I am a microbiologist with a background in microbial ecology, evolutionary biology and bioinformatics. I am interested in how microorganisms adapt to and survive in extreme environments, and how they shape the ecosystems around them. My research focuses on cold-adapted bacteria from the Arctic and Antarctica. In my MSc work, I compared the genomes of plant-associated bacteria from both poles that can harvest light, including their bacteriochlorophyll and photosynthetic reaction center genes, as well as their cold-adaptation and carbon metabolism. This showed which traits are shared across the poles and which have diverged.

Currently, my doctoral research builds on this work. I study how the first microbes colonize ground newly exposed by retreating ice on the Basen nunatak in Antarctica. I ask how chance and the environment shape these pioneer communities, whether mosses and algae select their own microbes, and which genes help microbes survive cold, drying, UV radiation and darkness. To answer these questions, I combine field samples with DNA sequencing, genome analysis and laboratory experiments. Together, this work helps explain how microbial life establishes itself in a warming polar world.

Beyond research, I mentor students in microbiology and bioinformatics, especially those working on thesis/dissertation projects in under-resourced settings. I care deeply about fair scientific collaboration between institutions in the Global North and South. My long-term goal is to help strengthen research capacity worldwide through interdisciplinary training and equitable access to genomic and microbiological science.
